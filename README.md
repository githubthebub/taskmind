# The Long Game

*A money-mindset game for the modern economy — thirty years, one sitting.*

The kitchen-table money games of the 90s (Cashflow / *Rich Dad Poor Dad*) taught a generation one big idea: **assets that pay you are the exit from the rat race.** The Long Game keeps that core loop and rebuilds everything around it for today — gig income, buy-now-pay-later, meme-asset FOMO, AI-shaped careers, subscription creep, 1.5% "wealth managers" — with every lesson drawn from published behavioral-economics and wellbeing research, cited in-game as collectible **Field Notes**.

## Play

Open `index.html` in any browser. No build, no dependencies, no network — one self-contained file.

## How it works

- **One year per turn.** Your salary, spending, debts, and markets tick; then life deals you one decision card.
- **Win condition — escape velocity:** income from assets (index funds, a systematized business, interest) covering the full cost of your life. The end-screen hero is the *crossover chart* — the year the passive-income line crosses the cost-of-living line.
- **But the game grades four things**, because the research says maximizing only money is losing: **Freedom, Wealth, Wellbeing, Wisdom.** You can retire rich and hollow; the game will notice.
- **Three starting hands:** The Graduate (debt + decades), The Freelancer (volatile income, no floor), The Restart (age 38, shorter runway).
- **The ledger is always live:** move money between cash, emergency fund, index funds, and speculation, pay down debts, and set an auto-invest rate that fires every year without asking — automation is both a mechanic and the lesson.

## The research inside

Every decision card maps to a documented result, unlocked as a cited Field Note (22 total). A sample:

| Mechanic in the game | Research it teaches |
|---|---|
| Crash years tempt you to sell; holding/buying wins on average | Loss aversion — Kahneman & Tversky (1979); Barber & Odean (2000); missing the 10 best days ≈ half the return |
| Auto-invest rate beats "I'll invest what's left" | Thaler & Benartzi, *Save More Tomorrow* (2004) |
| The 1.5%-fee "wealth manager" quietly drags returns | S&P SPIVA scorecards: ~90% of active funds trail the index over 15 years |
| Raises whisper "upgrade the lifestyle" | Hedonic adaptation — Brickman & Campbell; Frederick & Loewenstein |
| Pay-in-4 feels painless by design | Pain of paying / payment decoupling — Prelec & Loewenstein (1998) |
| Emergency fund turns crises into annoyances | Federal Reserve SHED; Pew (2015) on small buffers |
| Windfalls get spent loosely | Mental accounting — Thaler (1999) |
| Speculation capped at 5% can't hurt the plan | Lottery-like assets underperform — Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw (2011) |
| Reskilling when AI eats 40% of your role | Returns to human capital — Psacharopoulos & Patrinos (2018) |
| Trips beat televisions; giving beats both | Van Boven & Gilovich (2003); Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008) |
| Golden handcuffs and burnout have a price | Time affluence — Whillans et al., PNAS (2017) |
| The wellbeing grade exists at all | Harvard Study of Adult Development (Waldinger & Schulz) |

## Honest simplifications

All amounts are in today's dollars (returns are real, after inflation). Taxes, mortgages, and asset-location details are abstracted; the buy-the-dip rebound bonus is a small pedagogical thumb on the scale for a real effect (best days cluster near worst days). This is a flight simulator for judgment, not financial advice.
